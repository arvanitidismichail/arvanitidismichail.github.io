Research

About

Dr Michail Arvanitidis (PT, PhD, MSc, MMACP) is a Postdoctoral Research Fellow at the Centre of Precision Rehabilitation for Spinal Pain (CPR Spine), School of Sport, Exercise and Rehabilitation Sciences, University of Birmingham, United Kingdom. His current research examines cervical adaptations in NASA astronauts following spaceflight, aiming to enhance astronaut health for future missions. His main interests involve studying motor adaptations in pain-affected or vulnerable populations.

The neck in space

The cervical spine: seven vertebrae, C1 to C7, separated by discs (teal) and held in a gentle forward curve by the deep neck muscles (red).

Without gravity to compress it, the spine lengthens. The discs take on fluid and swell, the neck's curve flattens, and the deep neck muscles, no longer holding up the head, can lose size. The Cervical in Space trial measures these changes in astronauts, and how they relate to the higher risk of disc herniation after flight.

High-density EMG, live

A grid of 64 electrodes over a muscle: each one records its own signal. Their amplitude becomes a colour map, blue where the muscle is quiet and red where it works hardest, and in 3D a landscape of activity. Move across the grid to shift it. Changes in this spatial distribution are one way muscles adapt to pain, and a recurring focus of this research.

Simulated signal, for illustration.

Motor units, live

A motor unit is one motor neuron in the spinal cord and the muscle fibres it drives. Each time the neuron fires, an action potential runs down its axon and all of its fibres respond together; added up, they make the EMG recorded on the skin. The raster shows the same units as rows of discharges, as identified from high-density EMG. Raise the force to recruit more units and make them fire faster. Switch on pain to see the pattern reported in people with pain: slower, less regular firing and a less steady force.

Simulated, for illustration. Sound plays each discharge as a note.
